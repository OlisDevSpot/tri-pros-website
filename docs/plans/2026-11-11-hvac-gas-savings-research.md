# How much of the whole gas bill a new furnace and new ducts save (research, 2026-11-11)

**For:** the proposal bill-cut figures. The tool shows a cut as a flat percent of the WHOLE gas bill. Today it claims 20% for a new HVAC system plus 5% (compounded) for new ducts. The electric side is settled and is not covered here, except where the furnace blower touches it.
**How it was made:** web research on 2026-11-11 against the owners' own documents: DOE's Federal Register rule, CEC RASS 2019 reports, SoCalGas rate alerts and CPUC rate-case testimony, CEC and CPUC code and measure documents, and LBNL, ORNL and CEC field studies. Nothing was built. Figures marked **secondary, unverified** came only from trade press or third-party pages. The arithmetic is shown so each input can be swapped.

**Bottom line.** Space heating is about **one third of a SoCalGas home's annual gas bill** (water heating is the bigger half), and the $5 monthly customer charge is about 7% that no cut can touch. So a furnace only moves the whole bill by its saving on heating gas times about 0.33. Against the old system the owner already assumes (about 8 SEER A/C, so a pre-1992 system, whose furnace runs at about 67% in a mild climate), the honest whole-bill cuts are: **(a) new 80% furnace about 5%** (coastal 5%, inland 6%); **(b) new 95–96% condensing furnace about 10%** (coastal 9%, inland 10%); **(c) 95% furnace plus new sealed R-6/R-8 attic ducts about 14%** (coastal 13%, inland 15%). The duct step alone is worth about 5% of the bill on top of a new furnace, so **the current "+5% compounded" for ducts holds up. The "20%" for the system does not.** 20% is only reached by a 1950s–70s original furnace with a standing pilot left lit all year (about 22–25% there). It is under 1% for an 80% furnace replacing a 1990s–2000s 80% furnace. **Recommended defaults: 10% for a new 95%+ furnace (5% if an 80% unit is installed), then 5% compounded for new ducts, about 14.5% combined.** From 18 Dec 2028 every new non-weatherized gas furnace made must be 95% AFUE, unless the pending delay or the court remand changes that.

Sections: **1. What share of the bill is heating** · **2. What the old furnace really does** · **3. What a new furnace saves** · **4. What the old ducts lose** · **5. Whole-bill arithmetic** · **6. What changes the answer** · **Open / could not verify**.

## 1. What share of a SoCalGas bill is space heating

### 1.1 Therms by end use (CEC RASS 2019)

RASS 2019 is the CEC's survey of about 40,000 homes, with end-use therms estimated from metered bills (conditional demand analysis).

| Figure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Statewide gas use per home | 360 therms/yr; space heating 32%, water heating 59%, cooking 5%, dryer 2%, pools/spas/misc 2% | [RASS 2019 Executive Summary, Fig. ES-4](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-08/CEC-200-2021-005-ES.pdf) |
| SoCalGas homes with gas data, all dwelling types | Household total 363 therms. Primary heat 128 therms (76% of homes have it). Water heat 267 (84%). Range/oven 26. Dryer 13 | [RASS 2019 Vol. 2 Results, Table 36](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-08/CEC-200-2021-005-RSLTS.pdf); same figures in the [CEC staff presentation, TN 238346](https://efiling.energy.ca.gov/getdocument.aspx?tn=238346) |
| Single-family vs all homes, statewide | Primary heat 191 vs 158 therms (ratio 1.21). Water heat 260 | Vol. 2 Results, Table 33; Executive Summary Table ES-4 |
| SoCalGas single-family, homes built before 2013 | Household total 432 therms/yr | Vol. 2 Results, Table 35 |

RASS does not publish a SoCalGas single-family heating figure. Scaling SoCalGas's 128 therms by the statewide single-family ratio gives **about 155 therms of heating in a 432-therm single-family home: 36% of therms**. For the usual home with gas heat, gas water heater, gas range and gas dryer, the sum is 155 + 260 + 25 + 12 = 452 therms, and heating is 34%.

Cross-check from SoCalGas's own meter data. The average non-CARE residential customer used 65 therms in January and 19 in August–September (2020–2024), 36 a month on average, about 432 a year ([SoCalGas/SDG&E rate design testimony, M. Foster, A.25-09-014, Table MF-10](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/PublishedDocs/SupDoc/A2509014/9390/608671188.pdf)). If every therm above the summer low were heating, heating would be 432 − 12 × 19 = 204 therms, 47%. That is an upper bound, because water heating also rises in winter as the inlet water gets colder. The RASS-based 36% sits inside it.

### 1.2 Dollars, and the part a cut cannot touch

- **Average bill.** SoCalGas's March 2026 rate alert: a typical non-CARE customer uses 35 therms a month and pays **$72.39 a month**, about $869 a year. The bundled average rate is about $2.10 per therm ([SoCalGas Rate Alert, March 2026](https://www.socalgas.com/sites/default/files/2026-04/Natural-Gas-Rates-March2026.pdf)).
- **Customer charge.** $5 a month, billed as $0.16438 per meter per day ([Foster testimony, p. MF-2 and footnote 5](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/PublishedDocs/SupDoc/A2509014/9390/608671188.pdf)). That is $60 a year, **about 7% of the average bill**. A cut cannot touch it.
- **Climate credit.** $36.06 per household in 2026, on the April bill (same rate alert). It lowers the bill once a year and does not change with usage.
- **Tiers.** Winter therms fall mostly above the baseline allowance, so they are priced at the higher non-baseline rate. A heating cut therefore saves slightly more per therm than the average rate. I could not verify the 2026 tier rates (see Open), so the arithmetic below uses the average rate, which slightly understates heating dollars.

**Heating share of the whole annual bill ≈ 36% of therms × 93% volumetric ≈ 33%.**

### 1.3 Inland vs coastal

The evidence says the split is small.

- **Degree days.** Long-term heating degree days (base 65 °F) from the Title 24 design data are nearly the same inland and on the coast: LAX 1,819; Torrance 1,859; Santa Monica 1,873; Long Beach AP 1,606; Santa Ana 1,430; Costa Mesa 1,482; Riverside 1,818; San Bernardino 1,777; Ontario 1,710; Redlands 1,993; Pomona 1,971 ([Title 24 JA2 Table 2-3, Design Day Data for California Cities](https://energycodeace.com/site/custom/public/reference-ace-2019/Documents/table23designdaydataforcaliforniacities1.htm)). What differs is the shape. Inland winter nights are colder (winter median of extremes 26–29 °F vs 32–39 °F on the coast) and the daily swing is double, so inland heating comes in cold mornings and coastal heating comes in many cool, mild days. A few entries in that table look wrong (Moreno Valley 611, Northridge 650, Van Nuys 664), so I ignored them.
- **SoCalGas bills by baseline zone.** SoCalGas's own average-use bill is $84.98 a month in its Climate Zone 1 and $86.52 in Zone 2, only 2% apart; Zone 3 (mountain and high desert) is $109.48 ([Foster testimony, Table MF-8](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/PublishedDocs/SupDoc/A2509014/9390/608671188.pdf), at illustrative rates). The 2002 baseline allowances also put Zone 2's winter allowance only 8% above Zone 1's: 1.823 vs 1.691 therms per day ([SDG&E/SoCalGas SB 711 testimony, 2020 TCAP, Table 1](https://www.sdge.com/sites/default/files/regulatory/Ch%2013%20Chaudhury%20SB%20711%202020%20TCAP%20_0.pdf)). Which cities sit in Zone 1 vs Zone 2 could not be verified.
- **RASS by forecasting zone.** Primary-heat therms in the Southern California forecasting zones run from 100 to 145: zone 7 108–110, zone 8 143, zones 10 and 11 132–137, LADWP zones 16 and 17 106–128, SDG&E zone 12 100 ([Vol. 2 Results, Tables 38–40](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2021-08/CEC-200-2021-005-RSLTS.pdf)). The CEC labels zones 10 and 11 "desert". The report does not name the zones, so which one is the Inland Empire could not be verified.
- **Real coastal homes vary hugely.** In a CEC field study of five 1951–1976 ranch homes in Costa Mesa, Covina, Lake Forest, Northridge and Rancho Palos Verdes, modeled heating at 68 °F with typical weather was 23, 37, 34, 133 and 347 therms a year before retrofit ([CEC-500-2023-024, Table 19](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2023-05/CEC-500-2023-024.pdf)). A tight coastal home barely heats; a leaky one with an original furnace burns more on heat than on water.

**Used below:** heating share of the whole bill **30% coastal (range 22–35%)**, **35% inland (range 30–42%)**, **33% blended default.**

## 2. What the old furnace really does

### 2.1 Rated efficiency by era

| Era of the furnace now in the attic | Typical rating | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1950s to late 1970s: natural draft, standing pilot | 56–70% AFUE ("old, low-efficiency") | [DOE Energy Saver home heating sheet](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2014/01/f6/homeHeating.pdf) |
| Late 1970s to 1991: natural draft, no standing pilot in California (see 2.3) | Below 78%; no federal minimum before 1992 | DOE rule history in [88 FR 87502](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-12-18/html/2023-25514.htm): NAECA (1987) set "a minimum AFUE of… 78 percent" for furnaces made from 1 Jan 1992 |
| 1992 to Nov 2015 | 78% minimum; most sold at 80% (induced draft) | Same |
| Nov 2015 on | 80% minimum for non-weatherized gas furnaces, "manufactured on or after November 19, 2015" | Same, 10 CFR 430.32(e)(1)(ii) as amended |
| From 18 Dec 2028 | 95% (see 3.3) | Same |

The CEC field study above found the same mix in real Southern California attics. Pre-retrofit furnaces were rated 80%, 81%, 78%, 95.5%, and one "original 1950s furnace" estimated at 60% AFUE (Rancho Palos Verdes, 125 kBtu/h for 1,779 ft²) ([CEC-500-2023-024, Tables 5–9](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2023-05/CEC-500-2023-024.pdf)).

### 2.2 Real-world efficiency after decades

- **Age itself costs little.** ORNL tested old furnaces in the field: "The effects of aging and maintenance (or lack of maintenance) were not significant in the three old furnaces tested. The steady-state, full-load efficiency in the 'as-found' condition was comparable to that of the new furnaces" ([ORNL-NSF-EP-88, Hise & Holman, 1975, §2.1](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/5085082)). The losses that matter are pilot, cycling, oversizing and ducts, not a tired heat exchanger. A cracked heat exchanger is a safety problem, not an efficiency one.
- **Mild climates punish old furnaces.** ORNL computed the seasonal efficiency of a 75%-steady-state, standing-pilot, atmospheric furnace by city. **San Diego: 52.8% correctly sized, 48% at twice oversize** (combustion air from outdoors), 51.4% and 46.3% when it draws air from the house. Philadelphia was 63.8% and Minneapolis 66.4% (ORNL Table 3.3). The milder the winter, the bigger the share of fuel the pilot and the off-cycle losses eat.
- **Oversizing is the norm.** "A furnace that is oversized by a factor of 2, which is not uncommon, will require 8 to 10% more fuel" (ORNL §2.1). The 1950s Rancho Palos Verdes furnace was 125 kBtu/h against a 40 kBtu/h replacement.

**Used below: era A (standing pilot) 50% seasonal; era B (pre-1992, no pilot) 67%, range 60–72%; era C (1992–2015) 78%.** The era B figure comes from ORNL's 52.8% for San Diego with the pilot's standby share (about a fifth of fuel in a climate that mild) taken out. It also sits in DOE's 56–70% band for old natural-draft units.

### 2.3 Standing pilots

- **Burn rate.** "Furnace pilots measured consume 1 to 1.3 cfh of gas." During standby "the energy of the pilot gas is completely lost." In Philadelphia the heating-season pilot was 9.9% of seasonal gas ([ORNL §3.1.4, Table 3.2](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/5085082)). In a SoCal climate the share is larger because the heating load is small.
- **Per year.** 1–1.3 cfh is about 1,030–1,340 Btu/h: **about 90–117 therms if lit all year**, and **about 45–59 therms over May–October alone** (about 4,400 hours). Many owners never turn it off; ORNL's first recommendation was "Turn the pilot off during nonheating seasons."
- **How many are left.** California bars a "continuously burning pilot light" on fan-type central furnaces ([Title 24 §110.5](https://energycodeace.com/content/cy25-section-1105-natural-gas-central-furnaces-cooking-equipmen); the underlying statute is [Pub. Res. Code §25960](https://california.public.law/codes/public_resources_code_section_25960)). The statute's source note is dated 1978. The exact date the ban began to apply to new central furnaces could not be verified. So a standing pilot on a central furnace now means an original furnace from roughly the late 1970s or before. Wall and floor furnaces are not fan-type central furnaces and may still have one.

## 3. What a new furnace saves

### 3.1 The rule

At the same heat delivered, gas saved = 1 − (old efficiency ÷ new efficiency). The ducts and the house load cancel out of this ratio. 80% → 95% is 1 − 80/95 = 15.8% (rating arithmetic, not a field figure).

| Old furnace (seasonal) | New 80% AFUE | New 95% AFUE |
|---|---|---|
| A. Standing pilot, 50% | 37.5% of heating gas, plus the off-season pilot (45–59 therms) if it was left lit | 47.4%, plus the off-season pilot |
| B. Pre-1992, no pilot, 67% (60–72%) | 16.3% (10–25%) | 29.5% (24–37%) |
| C. 1992–2015, 78% | 2.5% | 17.9% |

New-unit inputs: 80% and 95% are the ratings. A condensing furnace reaches its rating when return air is cool, and SoCal return air sits around 65–70 °F, so a large field penalty is not expected. I did not find a field study measuring installed AFUE in California attics (see Open).

### 3.2 Field evidence from Southern California

The CEC study fitted 97.5% AFUE, two-stage, 14 ng/J ultra-low-NOx furnaces in five 1951–1976 LA and Orange County homes, together with R-38 attic insulation, R-8 buried ducts and, in two homes, new duct systems and attic air sealing ([CEC-500-2023-024](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2023-05/CEC-500-2023-024.pdf)):
- Modeled heating-gas savings, typical weather, 68 °F: 6.6% (Costa Mesa, already an 80% furnace and a 1992 retrofit), 34%, 66%, 43%, 71% (the 60% original furnace). Average 44% (Table 19).
- Bill-based savings, weather-normalized: simple average 12% against all prior years and 22% against the last "clean" year; 38% and 33% without the one site whose occupancy rose (Fig. 9).
- Heating-mode blower electricity was only 17–97 kWh a year before retrofit, so the furnace fan barely matters on the electric side in heating. Its cooling-mode role belongs to the settled electric figure.

These results bracket the table: little saving when the old unit is already 80%, very large saving when it is an original 1950s furnace.

### 3.3 Can a horizontal attic furnace be condensing, and what the 2023 DOE rule means

- **Yes, in practice.** The CEC study installed 97.5% condensing furnaces in vented attics of 1950s–70s ranch homes. At the Northridge home the ducts lay on the attic joists around the furnace, so that unit was in the attic. DOE's analysis "accounts for attic installations" and includes "heat tape typical in unconditioned attic installations" for freeze protection ([88 FR 87502](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-12-18/html/2023-25514.htm)).
- **What it takes.** New PVC intake and exhaust through the roof or a gable instead of the old metal flue. A condensate drain to an approved point. In an attic, protection against a blocked drain (an auxiliary pan with its own drain, or a cut-off switch) under California Mechanical Code §310 ([CMC §310.1](https://gocodebook.com/library/us/ca/ca-mechanical-code/chapter-3-general-regulations/310.1-condensate-disposal.-condensate-from-air-washers-air-cooling-coils-condensing-appliances), secondary text host; [City of Campbell 2022 summary](https://www.campbellca.gov/DocumentCenter/View/20105/2022-CONDENSATE-DISPOSAL-REQUIREMENTS)). Freezing is rarely a concern in coastal and valley attics; in the mountains and high desert it is. If the old furnace shared a flue with an atmospheric water heater, that water heater's vent must be re-checked once the furnace leaves the flue. The industry groups' objections in the rule record were about exactly these vent and drain costs in existing homes.
- **The rule.** Non-weatherized gas furnaces must meet **95% AFUE**, and compliance "is required on and after December 18, 2028" ([88 FR 87502](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-12-18/html/2023-25514.htm); [DOE Consumer Furnaces page](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/buildings/consumer-furnaces)). The standard binds furnaces *manufactured* on or after that date (10 CFR 430.32(e)), so 80% units made before it can still be installed from stock.
- **It may move.** On 27 April 2026 DOE published for comment a gas-industry petition to push the compliance date to 1 January 2030 ([91 FR 22477](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/27/2026-08145/energy-conservation-program-notification-of-petition-for-rulemaking)). The D.C. Circuit upheld the rule on 4 Nov 2025; in June 2026 the Supreme Court vacated that decision and sent it back "in light of the position" of the administration (**secondary, unverified**: [Contracting Business](https://www.contractingbusiness.com/industry-news/news/55383087/supreme-court-vacates-ruling-on-95-furnace-efficiency-standard), [ACCA](https://hvac-blog.acca.org/u-s-supreme-court-reopens-non-condensing-furnace-fight-in-win-for-fuel-and-technology-choice/)). DOE's page lists no withdrawal as of today.
- **For the tool.** Until the rule bites, an 80% horizontal attic furnace is still the cheap install, and its figure is half the 95% figure. After it bites, figure (b) is the only gas-furnace case.
- **Local NOx rule.** In the South Coast AQMD, furnaces must meet 14 ng/J ultra-low NOx (Rule 1111). The CEC study's 97.5% unit met it. Whether 80% ultra-low-NOx horizontal units are still widely stocked was not checked. SCAQMD staff proposed zero-emission amendments in 2024–25 ([PAR 1111, Feb 2025 working group](https://www.aqmd.gov/docs/default-source/rule-book/Proposed-Rules/1111-and-1121/par-1111-and-1121-wgm8-february-2025.pdf)); their outcome could not be verified.

## 4. What the old attic ducts lose, and what new sealed R-6/R-8 ducts save

- **Typical old leakage.** The CPUC's deemed duct-sealing measures model pre-2006 single-family homes at a "High" baseline of 40% total leakage (of fan flow) or a "Medium" 24%, and call a system "sealed" only at 5% or less, the Title 24 definition ([CPUC Energy Division memo on duct sealing measures, 16 Aug 2022](https://cedars-3.cpuc.ca.gov/deer-resources/deemed-measure-packages/guidance/file/2914/download)). The CEC field homes measured 30% and 40% total leakage before retrofit (Northridge, Rancho Palos Verdes) ([CEC-500-2023-024, Tables 8–9](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2023-05/CEC-500-2023-024.pdf)).
- **Measured losses and savings, California.** LBNL tested 24 Sacramento homes, almost all with attic ducts, before and after sealing with mastic and wrapping in R-6. Delivery efficiency rose **from 64% to 76%**. Supply-leak losses fell from 18% to 8%. Supply conduction stayed about 16%. HVAC energy use fell **18% on average (range 5–57%)** ([Jump, Walker & Modera, LBNL, ACEEE Summer Study 1996](https://www.aceee.org/files/proceedings/1996/data/papers/SS96_Panel1_Paper15.pdf)). Earlier studies they cite found losses "on the order of 35%", and a 16% cut in heating energy in another pre/post study.
- **Physics, older.** ORNL: uninsulated attic ducts can lose "40% of a furnace output under mild conditions", and leaky ducts "can lose almost any amount of heat" ([ORNL 1975, §2.1](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/5085082)).
- **What new ducts must meet.** For an entirely new or complete replacement duct system (at least 75% new material) the 2025 code requires a tested leakage of **5% or less**, and duct insulation of **R-6 in climate zones 3 and 5–7, R-8 in 1, 2, 4 and 8–16** ([CEC CF1R-ALT-02-E, 2025 Single-Family, effective 1 Jan 2026](https://www.energy.ca.gov/filebrowser/download/8301?fid=8301)). For Tri Pros' area that is R-6 in coastal zones 6–7 and R-8 in zones 8, 9 and 10. So the "R-6/R-8" in the scope is simply the code minimum.
- **A furnace alone already triggers duct testing.** The same form: when heating or cooling components are installed in a ducted system, a duct leakage test is required, and the ducts must reach 10% or less, 7% or less to outside, or have all accessible leaks sealed. Ducts "constructed, insulated, or sealed with asbestos" and ducts already registered as sealed are exempt. A permitted furnace-only job therefore already captures part of the duct saving, which shrinks the extra gain from full replacement. The asbestos exemption means many 1950s–60s systems get no sealing unless the ducts are replaced.
- **Saving used below.** A full replacement goes further than LBNL's seal-and-wrap (5% vs about 8% leakage after, R-8 vs R-6 over old material), but the furnace-only job's sealing takes part of it. **Used: 18% of heating gas (range 10–25%).** Lower when the existing ducts are 1990s flex in fair shape (CPUC "Medium" case), higher for original metal ducts with failed or asbestos wrap.

## 5. Whole-bill arithmetic

**Whole-bill cut = heating share of the bill (H) × saving on heating gas.** H = 0.30 coastal, 0.35 inland, 0.33 default (§1). Furnace + ducts compound: 1 − (old ÷ new) × (1 − 0.18).

| Case | Heating gas saved | Coastal (H 0.30) | Inland (H 0.35) | Default (H 0.33) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Old = B, pre-1992, 67% (matches an 8 SEER A/C era)** | | | | |
| (a) new 80% | 16.3% | 4.9% | 5.7% | **5.4%** |
| (b) new 95% | 29.5% | 8.8% | 10.3% | **9.7%** |
| (c) new 95% + new ducts | 42.2% | 12.7% | 14.8% | **13.9%** |
| (c) new 80% + new ducts | 31.3% | 9.4% | 11.0% | 10.3% |
| **Old = C, 1992–2015, 78%** | | | | |
| (a) new 80% | 2.5% | 0.8% | 0.9% | 0.8% |
| (b) new 95% | 17.9% | 5.4% | 6.3% | 5.9% |
| (c) new 95% + new ducts | 32.7% | 9.8% | 11.4% | 10.8% |
| **Old = A, standing pilot, 50%** | | | | |
| (a) new 80% | 37.5% | 11.2% | 13.1% | 12.4% |
| (b) new 95% | 47.4% | 14.2% | 16.6% | 15.6% |
| (c) new 95% + new ducts | 56.8% | 17.1% | 19.9% | 18.8% |
| A, plus a pilot left lit May–Oct (45–59 therms of a 432-therm year × 93% volumetric) | — | add about 10–13 points | add about 10–13 points | add about 10–13 points |

Worked example for the default, (c): 1 − (0.67 ÷ 0.95) × 0.82 = 1 − 0.705 × 0.82 = 0.422. Then 0.422 × 0.33 = 0.139, about 14% of the whole bill.

**Ducts as a compounded step after the furnace (default, B):** after a 95% furnace the bill is 0.903 of before. Heating is then 0.33 × 0.705 ÷ 0.903 = 26% of the new bill. 18% of that is **4.6%**, so "+5% compounded" is right.

**Ranges (default H, era B, low to high inputs):** (a) 3–9%; (b) 7–13%; (c) 10–18%.

**Recommended single defaults:**
- New furnace, 95%+ condensing: **10%** of the whole gas bill (inland 10%, coastal 9%).
- New furnace, 80%: **5%**.
- New sealed R-6/R-8 attic ducts: **+5% compounded** (unchanged).
- Combined 95% + ducts: 1 − 0.90 × 0.95 = **14.5%**.

In dollars at the March 2026 average bill ($869 a year): 10% ≈ $87, 14.5% ≈ $126, 5% ≈ $43 a year. Gas savings alone rarely pay for a furnace here; comfort, safety and the A/C side carry the case. The CEC study put the average for existing homes at $166 a year, gas and electricity together, at 2021 prices.

## 6. What changes the answer

- **Gas water heater and stove (the usual case).** They are already in the denominator and are why heating is only a third. A home with an electric water heater would have heating near 60–70% of its gas bill, roughly doubling every figure. A gas pool or spa heater (5–7% of SoCalGas homes, about 180 and 40 therms a year, RASS Table 36) shrinks every figure.
- **Thermostat habits and mild winters.** Setback, a cold-tolerant household or a tight coastal home cut the heating therms, so H falls and the percent cut falls with it. The CEC homes ranged from 23 to 347 heating therms a year. A home that barely heats gets maybe a third of the default.
- **The fixed charge may quadruple.** SoCalGas and SDG&E propose raising the residential customer charge from $5 to **$12 in 2028 and $20 in 2029**, with lower per-therm rates to keep revenue neutral ([Foster testimony, A.25-09-014](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/PublishedDocs/SupDoc/A2509014/9390/608671188.pdf); pending, not adopted). At $20, the fixed part is about a quarter of an average bill, and every whole-bill percent above falls by about a fifth. A tool that shows "% of the whole bill" should re-check this when the CPUC decides.
- **Which furnace is really there.** The biggest swing is the old unit's era: under 1% to over 20% for the same new furnace. Reading the old nameplate (AFUE, standing pilot, input size) at the in-home visit would let the tool pick row A, B or C instead of assuming.
- **The owner's own bills.** The honest per-home figure is the homeowner's winter-over-summer excess. Heating ≈ (Dec–Mar therms) − 4 × (an Aug–Sep month × about 1.3 for colder winter water), divided by the year's therms. That replaces H with the real one.
- **Right-sizing.** A new furnace sized to the house (40 kBtu/h in the CEC homes, against 75–125 before) recovers part of ORNL's 8–10% oversizing penalty. That is inside the era figures above, not on top.
